These videos highlight how social media creates unrealistic standards that affect everyone's mental health and self-image.

Ready-to-Use Sentence Frames

Fill in the gaps with your own ideas. These frames give you instant B2+ structure.

🎤 Opening — Introducing the topic

Frame 1 — State the issue

Nowadays, there is growing concern about the impact of social media on body image, particularly among young people / teenagers.

Frame 2 — Connect to everyday life

It is becoming increasingly common to see heavily edited photos / unrealistic beauty standards on platforms such as Instagram and TikTok.

Frame 3 — Personal hook

I recently came across a video in which a well-known model proved that perfect photos are just about angles and lighting, and it really made me think about how easily we are deceived by social media.

💡 Developing the argument

Frame 4 — Cause & effect

One of the main reasons why so many people feel insecure about their appearance is that social media only shows an idealised version of reality.

Frame 5 — Adding a second point

What is more, society tends to judge people based on their looks, which can lead to low self-esteem and even mental health problems.

Frame 6 — Contrast / concession

Although some people argue that social media can be a positive space for self-expression, I believe that, in most cases, it puts enormous pressure on people to look a certain way.

Frame 7 — Example

A clear example of this is the fact that even professional models admit that their photos are not real. This proves that what we see online is often far from the truth.

🎯 Giving your opinion / Solutions

Frame 8 — Opinion

From my point of view, it is essential that we raise awareness about the dangers of comparing ourselves to unrealistic images.

Frame 9 — Suggestion

I firmly believe that schools and families should play a more active role in teaching young people to think critically about the content they consume online.

Frame 10 — Personal reflection

Personally, I think the most important thing is to accept ourselves the way we are rather than trying to meet impossible standards set by others.

🏁 Closing

Frame 11 — Wrap up

All in all, I would say that social media has a significant impact on how people perceive themselves, and we need to be more critical of what we see online.

Frame 12 — Final thought

Ultimately, true wellbeing comes from self-acceptance and healthy relationships, not from how many likes a photo gets.

Key Vocabulary for This Topic

📱 Social Media & Image

unrealistic standards n. pl.
Impossible ideals of beauty or success that nobody can truly achieve.
to edit / retouch (a photo) v.
To alter an image digitally to make it look better.
body image n.
How a person sees and feels about their own body.
to go viral v. phr.
To spread rapidly online, reaching millions.
filtered reality n.
A version of life shown through beauty or camera filters.
influencer culture n.
The social phenomenon of people following online personalities.
to curate (a feed) v.
To carefully select what to post, showing only the best moments.
the highlight reel n.
Only the best parts of someone's life shown on social media.

🧠 Wellbeing & Mental Health

self-esteem n.
How much you value yourself; your sense of self-worth.
peer pressure n.
The influence from people your age to behave in a certain way.
to feel insecure about v. phr.
To lack confidence regarding something (appearance, ability…).
self-acceptance n.
Being happy with who you are, including your imperfections.
mental health awareness n.
The effort to educate people about psychological wellbeing.
to compare yourself to v. phr.
To look at others and feel worse or better about yourself.

⚖️ Society & Stereotypes

beauty standards n. pl.
The socially accepted ideas of what looks attractive.
to be judged on v. phr.
To be evaluated based on something (looks, weight, etc.).
societal pressure n.
The expectations that society places on individuals.
to conform to v. phr.
To behave or look the way society expects you to.
to challenge stereotypes v. phr.
To question and fight fixed, oversimplified ideas about people.
to raise awareness v. phr.
To make people more conscious of an issue or problem.
💡 B2+ tip: Don't just use simple adjectives. Upgrade your language: instead of saying "bad," say harmful, damaging, detrimental. Instead of "good," try beneficial, positive, rewarding. Examiners notice this range!

Model Monologue — How to Tackle This Topic

📝 "Body Image & Social Media Pressure"

Nowadays, there is growing concern about the impact of social media on our wellbeing, particularly when it comes to body image and beauty standards. Every day, we are bombarded with carefully curated images that show an idealised version of reality — perfect skin, perfect bodies, perfect lives. But how real is any of that?

I recently came across a video of a well-known model who showed how the same body can look completely different depending on the angle, the lighting, and the pose. What struck me the most was that even she admitted that the "perfect body" simply does not exist. It is nothing more than a combination of filters and retouching.

What is more, this problem goes beyond body image. Society tends to put people in boxes — you should look a certain way, dress a certain way, even think a certain way. Social media has made this worse because anyone who does not conform to these standards is often criticised or even bullied. As a result, many people, especially teenagers, end up feeling insecure about who they are.

Although some people argue that social media can be a positive space for self-expression and connecting with others, I believe that, in most cases, the pressure to look perfect outweighs the benefits. Not only does constant comparison damage self-esteem, but it also creates a culture where appearance matters more than who you really are.

From my point of view, the most important thing we can do is raise awareness. Schools should teach young people to think critically about the content they see online, and families should have open conversations about self-acceptance. If we all stopped comparing ourselves to filtered versions of other people, we would probably feel much happier and healthier.

To sum up, I firmly believe that true wellbeing comes from accepting ourselves the way we are, rather than trying to meet impossible standards set by society and social media. Ultimately, what makes us valuable is not how we look in a photo, but who we are as people.

📊 Why this works at B2+: It uses cleft sentences (What struck me the most…), inversion (Not only does…), a second conditional (If we all stopped…), varied connectors, and topic-specific vocabulary. It has a clear introduction → development → opinion → conclusion structure. It stays personal without being informal.

Self-Check Before Your Monologue

✅ Content & Structure

  • Did I introduce the topic clearly in my opening?
  • Did I give at least two developed points (not just list them)?
  • Did I include a specific example to support my ideas?
  • Did I express my personal opinion clearly?
  • Did I suggest a solution or reflect on what could change?
  • Did I conclude with a final thought that rounds off the monologue?

✅ Language Quality

  • Did I use at least 3 different connectors (not just "and", "but", "so")?
  • Did I include at least one B2+ grammar structure (cleft, inversion, passive reporting, conditional)?
  • Did I use topic-specific vocabulary (not just basic words)?
  • Did I avoid repeating the same word more than twice?
  • Did I vary my sentence length (some short, some complex)?

✅ Delivery (Speaking)

  • Did I speak fluently without too many long pauses?
  • Did I use natural fillers when needed (well, I mean, actually)?
  • Did I vary my intonation to keep the listener engaged?
  • Did I keep to the time limit (approx. 3–4 minutes)?
