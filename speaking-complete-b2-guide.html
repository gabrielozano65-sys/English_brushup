Test Overview

You take the test with another candidate (occasionally in a group of three), and there are two examiners: one speaks to you, the other only listens and assesses. The whole test lasts about 14 minutes.

PartTimeWho talksWhat you do
1 — Interview2 minExaminer ↔ each candidateAnswer personal questions about yourself: home, work/study, free time, plans, experiences
2 — Long turn4 minOne candidate at a timeCompare two photographs for 1 minute and answer a question about them. Your partner then gives a short (about 30 seconds) reaction to a question about your photos
3 — Collaborative task4 minThe two candidates togetherDiscuss written prompts for about 2 minutes, then decide something together in about 1 minute
4 — Discussion4 minExaminer and both candidatesDiscuss wider questions connected to the topic of Part 3
The golden rule Speaking is not a performance in front of an audience — it's a conversation. Examiners reward natural interaction, clear organisation and a good range of language, not perfect grammar or long memorised speeches.

★How You're Marked

One examiner scores you on four analytic criteria; the other gives you a global impression. Knowing what they look for tells you exactly what to practise.

CriterionWhat it meansHow to show it
Grammar & VocabularyRange and accuracy of structures and wordsMix tenses, use conditionals and passives, and choose precise vocabulary (delighted rather than very happy). Self-correct if you slip.
Discourse ManagementCoherence, organisation and length of what you sayDevelop each answer with a reason or example. Use linkers (however, as a result, for instance). Stay on topic.
PronunciationIndividual sounds, word stress, sentence stress and intonationStress the important words, vary your intonation and speak clearly. A foreign accent is fine — being understood is what counts.
Interactive CommunicationHow well you work with your partnerAsk for their opinion, react to what they say, invite them in, and keep the conversation moving without dominating it.
Global AchievementOverall effectiveness (second examiner)An overall impression of how well you handle the tasks across all four parts.

General strategy

  • Extend your answers. One-word answers lose marks. Use the formula answer + reason + example.
  • Never go silent. Use natural fillers (Let me think…, That's an interesting question…) rather than long pauses.
  • It's fine to ask for repetition. Sorry, could you repeat that, please? is perfectly acceptable — but don't rely on it every time.
  • Don't memorise speeches. Examiners notice and they can't give you credit for rehearsed text that doesn't answer the question.
  • Be a good partner. Looking at, listening to and reacting to your partner is part of your score.
  • Correct yourself calmly. I mean…, or rather… shows awareness and control.

Natural fillers vs. empty fillers

AvoidUse instead
“Eeeh… emmm… how do you say…”Let me think… · Well, I suppose… · That's a good question…
Long silence while searching for a wordWhat I mean is… · It's a kind of… · Something like… (paraphrase!)
“I don't know” and stoppingI've never really thought about it, but I'd say…

1Interview

About 2 minutes. The examiner asks each of you questions about your life. The aim is to relax and show you can talk about familiar topics.

What to expect

  • You'll be asked your name and where you're from, then questions about present (home, studies, work), past (a recent experience) and future (plans, hopes).
  • Answers should be 2–3 sentences, not one word — but not long speeches either.
  • Different tenses are your chance to show range: present simple, past simple, present perfect, going to / would like to.

Typical questions

TopicExamples
Home & placeWhere do you live? What do you like about living there? Would you like to live somewhere else?
Free timeWhat do you enjoy doing in your free time? How often do you do it? Have you taken up any new hobbies recently?
Work / studyWhat are you studying? What do you enjoy most about it? What would your ideal job be?
Past experiencesTell us about a memorable holiday. What did you do last weekend?
Future plansWhat are you hoping to do in the next few years? Do you think you'll travel more in future?

2Long Turn

About 4 minutes in total (two long turns). The examiner gives you two photographs and asks a question. You speak alone for one minute.

What to expect

  • You get two photographs, and a question printed above them that you should answer about both pictures.
  • The task is to compare the photos and answer the question — not to describe each photo in detail.
  • While you speak, your partner listens. Afterwards the examiner asks your partner a short question about your photos, and your partner answers in about 30 seconds.
  • When it's your partner's turn to speak, listen carefully — you'll be asked a short question about their photos.
The most common mistake Describing each photo separately for a minute and never answering the question. Spend a few seconds on similarities and differences, then use most of your time on the actual question.

A winning structure for one minute

StepTimeWhat to do
1. Compare~15 secOne clear similarity and one clear difference between the two photos.
2. Answer the question~35 secGive reasons for photo 1, then reasons for photo 2. Use speculation language.
3. Finish~10 secAdd a final comment. Stop when the examiner says "Thank you".

Useful language

FunctionPhrases
ComparingBoth photos show… · In the first photo…, whereas in the second… · One thing they have in common is… · Unlike the people in the first picture…
SpeculatingThey might / may / could be… · She must be… · He can't be… · It looks as if / as though… · They seem to be… · I'd say that…
Giving reasons…probably because… · …which might explain why… · …perhaps in order to…
Position in the photoin the foreground / background · on the left / right · in the middle · at the top / bottom
When you can't find a wordIt's a kind of… · something you use for… · I can't remember the word, but…
EndingAll in all, I'd say… · So, to sum up…

3Collaborative Task

About 4 minutes. You and your partner discuss a task together using written prompts. This is where interactive communication matters most.

What to expect

  • You see a central question and several options (usually five) in a diagram.
  • For about 2 minutes you discuss the question, talking about the options with your partner.
  • Then the examiner gives you a decision question and you have about 1 minute to reach a decision together.
  • You don't have to discuss all the options, and you don't have to agree — but you should try to reach a decision on the final question.
Remember This is a conversation between you and your partner, not an interview with the examiner. Look at your partner, not at the examiners, and respond to what they actually say.

Sample task

Task

Here are some things that can help teenagers stay healthy. Talk to each other about how important each of these is.

SleepDietExerciseFriendsHobbies

Decision question (1 minute): Now decide which two are the most important for teenagers to stay healthy.

    4Discussion

    About 4 minutes. The examiner asks questions to both of you, broadening the topic from Part 3. You can respond to the examiner and to each other.

    What to expect

    • Questions move from the specific topic to general opinions: society, the future, people in general.
    • The examiner may ask you individually, or ask both of you the same question.
    • Both of you should speak about equally. If your partner has been quiet, invite them in; if you're asked directly, answer fully.
    • It's fine to disagree with your partner, as long as you do it politely and give reasons.

    Typical questions

    Theme (linked to Part 3)Examples
    HealthDo you think people are healthier today than in the past? Should schools do more to teach young people about healthy living?
    TechnologyHow has technology changed the way people communicate? Do you think we depend too much on our phones?
    TravelIs it better to travel alone or with other people? Does tourism do more good than harm?
    EnvironmentWhat can individuals do to protect the environment? Whose responsibility is it — governments or ordinary people?
    Education & workIs it more important to enjoy your job or to earn a good salary? How will jobs change in the future?

    A strong answer: opinion + reason + example + balance

    Formula 1. Give your opinion → 2. Explain why → 3. Give an example → 4. Acknowledge the other side → 5. (Optionally) ask your partner what they think.

    Speculating about the futureI imagine that… · It's likely that… · I wouldn't be surprised if…

    ♪Pronunciation

    Pronunciation is one-fifth of your analytic marks. You don't need a native accent — you need to be easy to understand.

    AreaWhat it isTip
    Word stressThe syllable pronounced more strongly in a wordLearn the stress with every new word: PHOtograph, phoTOgraphy, photoGRAPHic.
    Sentence stressStressing the key content words in a sentenceStress nouns, main verbs and adjectives; say small words (of, the, to) quickly and weakly.
    IntonationThe rise and fall of your voiceFall at the end of statements; rise slightly to show you're not finished or when inviting your partner in.
    Connected speechSounds linking and changing between wordsSay "a lot of" as one flow, not three separate words.
    The schwa /ə/The most common English vowel, in unstressed syllablesSay comfortable as COM-fə-tə-bəl and about as ə-BOUT.

    Common challenges for Spanish speakers

    ChallengeExampleFix
    Adding /e/ before s + consonant“estudent” instead of student; “especial” instead of specialStart with a clean /s/ sound and link straight into the next consonant.
    Short vs. long vowelsship /ɪ/ vs. sheep /iː/; full vs. foolMake long vowels clearly longer, and short ones quick and relaxed.
    -ed endingswanted /ɪd/, worked /t/, played /d/Only add an extra syllable /ɪd/ after /t/ or /d/ (see exercise below).
    The /h/ soundhouse, have, howBreathe out gently: a light, soft /h/, not a harsh Spanish /x/.
    The “th” soundsthink /θ/, this /ð/Place the tongue lightly between the teeth — don't replace with /t/, /d/ or /s/.
    b / vvery vs. berryFor /v/, gently touch your top teeth to your lower lip.

        ✓Self-Check & Test Day

        Before you speak — checklist

        On test day

        • Listen carefully to the examiner's instructions — they are read from a fixed script, so understanding them is easy once you know the pattern.
        • Smile, make eye contact with your partner and speak at a natural pace.
        • If you make a mistake, correct yourself calmly and carry on — don't stop.
        • Don't worry about your partner's level. Be supportive; helping them participate improves your own interactive score.
        • Practise with a friend before the exam: record yourselves, play it back and note what to improve.
